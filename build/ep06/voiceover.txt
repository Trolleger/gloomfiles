[gloom] July 1518. A woman starts dancing in a street in Strasbourg. She doesn't stop.
[gloom] She danced for days, with no music, according to the accounts. Others joined. Within a week, dozens were dancing.
[gloom] By August, estimates say between 50 and 400 people. The figure is uncertain.
[gloom] Contemporary records, including a physician's notes, sermons and city council papers, do confirm the dancing.
[gloom] The council's first response was to encourage it. Guild halls were set aside, with musicians. It backfired. They then banned public dancing and music, and sent dancers to a shrine.
[gloom] Deaths are often reported, up to about 15 a day. But the Strasbourg sources from the time don't mention deaths. The claims come from later accounts.
[gloom] Theories: poisoning from mouldy grain (ergot), stress-induced mass illness, religious belief. One historian rejects ergot, because poisoned people couldn't dance for days. Stress and mass illness is the most popular view.
[gloom] It faded in early September. Nobody knows why it began.
