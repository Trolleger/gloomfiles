# Ep 4 source digest: Cicada 3301
Source: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cicada_3301 (read 2026-11-26; extraction missed the round-by-round section and puzzle message text).
1. Three puzzle sets posted under the name "3301", 2012 to 2014. First: January 4, 2012, ran nearly a month. Second: January 4, 2013. Third never solved.
2. Stated aim: recruit "highly intelligent individuals". Ultimate purpose unknown.
3. Topics: data security, cryptography, steganography, internet anonymity.
4. Clues online AND physical: images with hidden data, a telephone message, websites, paper signs with QR codes.
5. References to literature, poetry, art and music; works named in the puzzle include the poem Agrippa (A Book of the Dead), the Mabinogion, and The Book of the Law.
6. Every clue signed with the same OpenPGP key.
7. A 2013 solver (private person, don't name): finalists were asked about information freedom, online privacy and rejecting censorship; good answers got a private-forum invite and were told to build a project furthering the group's ideals.
8. Another winner documented it on a video channel.
9. 2012: Chilean police called it a "hacker group"; Cicada denied illegal activity in a signed statement. (Source headline ambiguous)
10. 2015: a group using "3301" hacked a health organisation's database; Cicada's signed statement: "not associated with this group in any way"; the hackers confirmed it too.
11. 2014: the US Navy released its own puzzle challenge modelled on it, "Project Architeuthis", to recruit cryptologists.
12. January 2016: a new clue appeared online; April 2017: last verified signed message, denying any unsigned puzzle.
13. Called "the most elaborate and mysterious puzzle of the Internet age" (Metro, 2013); Washington Post: among the "top 5 eeriest, unsolved mysteries of the Internet".
14. Theories: NSA, CIA, MI6, Mossad recruiting; a "Masonic conspiracy"; cyber mercenaries; a game (but nobody ever made money from it); a cult. All unverified.
15. Inspired a TV episode (Person of Interest, 2015) and a 2021 film.
