# Ep 7 source digest: Voynich manuscript
Source: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript (read 2026-11-26; last ~10% via summary, marked).
1. Yale Beinecke MS 408; calfskin, about 23.5 x 16.2 cm; about 240 pages survive (probably 272 originally; at least 14 folios missing, incl. the Aquarius and Capricorn pages).
2. Carbon-dated 1404-1438 (Arizona, 2009); not a palimpsest.
3. First confirmed mention: 1639 letter from Prague alchemist Georg Baresch to Athanasius Kircher (who claimed to have decoded hieroglyphs), enclosing a sample; Baresch called it a "Sphynx" taking up shelf space. Whether Kircher replied is unknown. Letter rediscovered 1999.
4. Passed to Jan Marek Marci; his 1665 cover letter (still attached) says Emperor Rudolf II paid 600 ducats (about 2 kg of gold) and someone suspected Roger Bacon. Rudolf's ownership "plausible, but unproven".
5. A faded name on page 1 visible under UV (a botanist of Rudolf's); possibly added later.
6. About 200 years of silence; later in Jesuit hands in Rome; hidden in private libraries when Italian troops took Rome in 1870.
7. 1912: book dealer Wilfrid Voynich bought it (with 29 other manuscripts) from the Jesuits at Villa Mondragone near Rome; spent 7 years trying to get it deciphered.
8. His widow Ethel (novelist, daughter of George Boole) kept it; 1961 a dealer bought it, couldn't sell it, gave it to Yale in 1969; online in full since 2020.
9. Sections: herbal (126 pp), astronomical, "balneological" (tiny nude women in green pools joined by pipes), cosmological (nine "islands" joined by causeways, castles, maybe a volcano, on a six-page foldout), pharmaceutical, recipes.
10. Plants mostly unidentifiable, some look like root-and-leaf mash-ups.
11. Zodiac wheels: 30 women each, holding stars on tethers.
12. About 37,900 words, 8,100 unique, 170,000+ characters; alphabet of 20-25 main characters; no punctuation; smooth writing with no hesitation.
13. Words repeat up to five times in a row; letters behave like real-language word endings; Zipf-like frequencies; but unusually predictable (low entropy).
14. About five different scribes (Davis).
15. 1940s: William Friedman's team put every line on IBM punch cards; his wife Elizebeth Friedman (1962): single-substitution attempts "doomed to utter frustration".
16. Claimed solutions: Greek shorthand under a microscope (1921, disproved: ink cracks); Latin diary (1943); Ukrainian (1978); Aztec Nahuatl (2014, conflicts with carbon date); Old Turkic, proto-Romance, Hebrew, German dialect [summary]. None accepted.
17. Hoax tests: text can be generated with a 1550s grille; human-written nonsense shares many statistics (2022).
