# Ep 3 source digest: Numbers stations
Sources: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numbers_station (body); en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincolnshire_Poacher_(numbers_station) (full). Read 2026-11-26.
1. Shortwave stations broadcasting formatted numbers, believed to be for intelligence officers abroad.
2. Format: a call-up ("¡Atención!", "Achtung!", "Ready? Ready?", "Charlie India Oscar"), then a message count, then groups of 4-5 digits, often read twice, then a sign-off ("End of message", some ex-Soviet ones end "00000").
3. Usually start on the hour or half hour (needs citation).
4. One-time pad: "theoretically unbreakable when applied correctly".
5. Shortwave bounces off the ionosphere, so one transmitter reaches the world; an ordinary radio gives the listener "plausible deniability".
6. Since at least WWI (then in Morse). Belief: a young Archduke Anton of Austria wrote numbers down and passed them to military intelligence.
7. Peak in the Cold War; West Germany the one confirmed one-time-pad case; Czechoslovakia's use acknowledged.
8. Some listeners who sent reception reports got reply cards back.
9. UK government spokesperson, 1998: "They are not for, shall we say, public consumption" and "These are what you suppose they are."
10. Lincolnshire Poacher: two-plus bars of the English folk song before every number string; a pre-recorded English female voice reading five-digit groups, the last digit of each group at a higher pitch.
11. Believed (not confirmed) MI6, transmitted from RAF Akrotiri in Cyprus (amateur direction-finding pointed to curtain antennas there). Origin site disputed (Gawcott vs Bletchley Park).
12. Last recorded transmission 2 July 2008. Sister station Cherry Ripe (own namesake song) ran to December 2009.
13. North Korea's state radio started broadcasting on one of its frequencies in 2006, possibly to jam it (citation needed).
14. Cuba's "Atención": first station publicly accused in court of sending to spies (US espionage trial; network arrested 1998). Prosecutors: agents copied numbers on Sony handheld shortwave radios and decoded them on laptops; the FBI had copied the decoding program from an apartment in 1995.
15. Decoded court exhibits included "Congratulate all the female comrades for International Day of the Woman."
16. 2010: the FBI said Russian sleeper agents also got coded shortwave instructions; a 2011 German case: a suspect "fell off her chair" with the radio cable as police arrived (reported).
17. 1983: a listener reported a woman reading numbers; experts said ski-slope snowfall figures; enthusiasts now think it was a numbers station.
18. 1990: a numbers station jammed a Caribbean air-traffic channel so badly a pilot had to switch frequency.
19. North Korea revived its number broadcasts in 2016 after 16 years of silence.
20. Hobbyists have logged them since the 1970s; the Conet Project (1997, 4 CDs) is the best-known archive.
