## EP 2: The Wow! Signal (funny)
Colour: violet space, cyan signal, amber printout. Music: "Comfortable Mystery", then "Lightless Dawn" from shot 7.

### Script
1. In 1977, a telescope listening for aliens caught 72 seconds of something. A man saw the printout and wrote one word: Wow!
2. Big Ear, in Ohio, couldn't turn sideways. It let Earth's spin drag the sky past, so every spot got exactly 72 seconds.
3. Days later, volunteer Jerry Ehman was checking the printouts by hand. He circled six characters, 6EQUJ5, and wrote "Wow!" *(Moth: "That's it? That's the whole reaction?")*
4. It's not a message. It's loudness. The U means about 30 times louder than the background, rising and falling exactly like a real point in the sky.
5. It sat near 1420 megahertz, the hydrogen line, the channel scientists had suggested in 1959 that aliens might use.
6. Big Ear had two horns. Three minutes later, the second should have caught it too. Nothing.
7. They've looked again ever since, as recently as 2022. Ehman: "We should have seen it again when we looked for it 50 times." *(Biscuit stares at the sky.)*
8. In 2012, someone replied anyway, from Arecibo, with about 10,000 tweets. The transmitter overheated.
9. Ehman still won't draw "vast conclusions from half-vast data". Gloom: "Neither will I. Mostly."

**New from the digest:** why it's 72 seconds, Ehman checking paper by hand, the second horn that heard nothing, the "50 times" quote, the 2012 tweet reply and its overheating transmitter.
**Cut:** the 2017 comet idea and its rejection, the 2024 hydrogen-cloud preprint, the 2022 candidate star 1,800 light-years away, the frequency typo dispute, Ehman's space-debris idea, Sagittarius, the pop-culture uses.

## EP 3: Numbers Stations (informational)
Colour: cold teal and black, one amber radio glow. Music: "Long Note Four", then "Cipher" from shot 6.

### Script
1. For decades, shortwave radio has carried voices reading numbers to someone. Governments almost never explain them.
2. Britain's most famous one opened with a few bars of an old folk song, "The Lincolnshire Poacher". Then an English woman's voice read five digits at a time, the last one always pitched higher.
3. It's believed, but never confirmed, to have been British intelligence. Hobbyists tracked the signal to antennas on a military base in Cyprus.
4. Asked about stations like it in 1998, a UK official said only: "These are what you suppose they are."
5. The leading explanation is a one-time pad. Only the listener with the matching pad can read each group. Used correctly, it can't be broken. And all the listener needs is an ordinary radio.
6. In a US spy trial, prosecutors said agents copied numbers from a Cuban station on handheld Sony radios, then decoded them on laptops.
7. One decoded order: congratulate all the female comrades for International Women's Day.
8. The Lincolnshire Poacher went silent in July 2008. Others are still on the air.
9. The numbers are public. The meaning isn't.

**New from the digest:** the higher-pitched last digit, the Cyprus antennas found by hobbyists, the official's "what you suppose they are", the Sony radios and laptops, the oddly ordinary decoded message.
**Cut:** call-ups like "Achtung!" and endings of "00000", WWI origins and the young Archduke, the Cold War West German case, reply cards sent to listeners, the 1983 "ski snowfall" mix-up, the 1990 air-traffic jam, Cherry Ripe, North Korea's 2016 revival, the Conet Project, the 2010 Russian sleeper agents.

## EP 4: Cicada 3301 (informational)
Colour: black and acid green, cold white light. Music: "Investigations", then "Darkest Child" from shot 7.

### Script
1. On January 4, 2012, an anonymous image appeared online, looking for "highly intelligent individuals". The test was hidden inside it.
2. Solving it led to a phone number, then websites, then paper posters with QR codes, out in the real world.
3. Clues came from old texts like the Welsh Mabinogion and Aleister Crowley's Book of the Law.
4. Every real clue was signed with the same cryptographic key, so fakes could be spotted.
5. One finalist said winners were quizzed on online privacy, information freedom and censorship. The best got an invite to a private forum, and a project to build.
6. It came back in 2013 and 2014. The third puzzle has never been solved.
7. The US Navy liked it enough to make its own puzzle, to recruit codebreakers.
8. Guesses: the NSA, the CIA, MI6, a secret society. Nobody has ever cashed in on it.
9. In 2015, hackers using the name 3301 broke into a database. The real group's signed reply: "not associated with this group in any way." Its last verified message came in April 2017.
10. It found the people it was looking for. What it wanted them for, nobody knows.

**New from the digest:** the posters in the real world, the named books, the private-forum "project", the Navy copying it, nobody ever profiting, the signed denial quote.
**Cut:** Chilean police calling it a "hacker group" (source unclear), the 2016 clue, the "most elaborate puzzle" press quotes, the TV episode and 2021 film, the Masonic and cult theories.

## EP 6: The Dancing Plague of 1518 (informational)
Colour: candle amber and woodcut brown, deep red at dusk. Music: "Village Consort", then "Echoes of Time" from shot 6.

### Script
1. July 14, 1518. Outside her half-timbered house in Strasbourg, a woman the chronicles call Frau Troffea starts to dance. No music. She keeps going for a week.
2. Her husband starts copying her. Within days, more than thirty. By August, between 50 and 400.
3. Accounts describe sweat-soaked bodies, thrashing arms, vacant eyes, and feet so swollen they bled into their shoes.
4. Physicians blamed overheated blood, and prescribed more dancing. The city reportedly opened guild halls, hired musicians, and strong men to hold dancers up.
5. It backfired. People feared Saint Vitus, said to punish sinners by making them dance, and more joined in.
6. So the council banned music and public dancing, and sent the dancers to his shrine, in red shoes marked with crosses and sprinkled with holy water.
7. Later accounts claim fifteen deaths a day. The city's own records from the time mention none.
8. Historian John Waller's leading theory: a mass stress illness in a city worn down by famine and disease. Not poisoned grain, he argues: poisoned people couldn't dance for days.
9. In September, the last dancers were led up to a mountain chapel. It stopped.

**New from the digest:** the date, Frau Troffea and her house, her husband copying her, the physical details, "overheated blood", the strong men, Saint Vitus, the red shoes and crosses, Waller's reasoning, the mountain chapel.
**Cut:** her daughter, the "four of six chronicles" detail, incense and Latin chants, "forgiven by Vitus", the river-route argument against ergot, earlier dancing outbreaks (Cölbigk, tarantism), the songs and films about it.

## EP 7: The Voynich Manuscript (informational)
Colour: parchment gold and leaf green, night blue for the star pages. Music: "Teller of the Tales", then "Mesmerize" from shot 7. Illustrations are redrawn originals, never copies of the pages.

### Script
1. In 1639, an alchemist in Prague wrote to a famous scholar about a book he couldn't read. He called it a Sphinx. Nobody has read it since.
2. It's about 240 pages of calfskin, carbon-dated to between 1404 and 1438.
3. A letter from 1665 claims Emperor Rudolf II once paid 600 gold ducats for it. That has never been proven.
4. In 1912, book dealer Wilfrid Voynich bought it from a Jesuit collection near Rome, and spent years trying to get scholars to crack it.
5. Inside: plants nobody can match, wheels of women holding stars on strings, and tiny bathers in green pools joined by pipes.
6. And about 38,000 words in an alphabet nobody knows. No punctuation. Some words repeat five times in a row.
7. In the 1940s, top US codebreaker William Friedman's team typed every line onto punch cards. They got nowhere.
8. Claimed solutions keep coming: Latin, Turkish, Hebrew, even Aztec. None has been accepted.
9. Today it's at Yale, and the whole book is online. Anyone can try.

**New from the digest:** the 1639 "Sphinx" letter, calfskin, the Rudolf II claim (attributed, unproven), the Jesuit sale, the women holding stars, words repeating five times, Friedman's punch cards, the parade of claimed solutions, the book being online.
**Cut:** Kircher and hieroglyphs, the faded name under UV, the 1870 hiding in Rome, Ethel Voynich, the missing zodiac pages, the six-page foldout, about five scribes, the statistics (8,100 unique words, low entropy), the hoax experiments, the Roger Bacon theory.
**Rudolf II:** kept as an attributed, unproven claim (Andrew, 2026-11-26).

## EP 8: Polybius (funny)
Colour: neon purple and arcade green on black. Music: "8bit Dungeon Level", then "Spy Glass" from shot 6.

### Script
1. The most dangerous arcade game ever made. Nobody has ever found one.
2. The story goes: 1981, Portland. A plain black cabinet called Polybius appears. Kids line up and fight to play it. *(Moth: "It sounds fun.")*
3. Then the claims: seizures, amnesia, nightmares. Men in black open the machines, collect data, and leave the coins inside.
4. Weeks later, every cabinet vanishes. The maker's name: Sinneslöschen, broken German for "sense delete". No such company existed.
5. The first trace isn't from 1981. It's a collectors' website page asking, "anyone heard of this game?" The first print mention: a magazine in 2003.
6. But some of it happened. In 1981, at one Portland arcade, a teen blacked out after playing Tempest. Another quit an Asteroids record attempt after 28 hours, with a stomach ache. *(Biscuit knocks a coin off the table.)*
7. And there really were men watching the machines: FBI agents, writing down high scores, before raiding arcades over illegal gambling machines.
8. One historian put it this way: what the Necronomicon is for books, Polybius is for video games.
9. The cabinet never existed. The legend is doing very well.

**New from the digest:** men in black leaving the coins, "sense delete", the "anyone heard of this game?" page, the two real kids and their games, the FBI writing down high scores, the Necronomicon line.
**Cut:** the cabinet switching itself on, the MKUltra link, the "Kyiv trip" update that never came, the self-claimed founder, Atari's secret test games, the 2016 VR game and its maker's hoax claim, the TV cameos, the legend's darkest endings (left out on purpose).

## EP 9: The Bloop (funny)
Colour: abyss blue and bioluminescent teal, icy white at the end. Music: "Atlantean Twilight", then "Ice Flow" from shot 8.

### Script
1. In 1997, the ocean made a sound so loud that microphones more than 3,000 kilometres apart heard it.
2. NOAA was listening for undersea volcanoes, with hydrophones added to the Navy's old network for hunting Soviet submarines.
3. The sound was ultra-low, rising in pitch for about a minute. They traced it to a lonely patch of the South Pacific, far west of South America.
4. They called it the Bloop. One NOAA scientist said it sounded alive, but "far more powerful than the calls made by any animal on Earth." *(Moth hides.)*
5. The theories poured in: secret military tests, giant squid, something bigger than any whale.
6. But that same scientist already had a hunch: ice.
7. NOAA kept moving its microphones closer to Antarctica. In 2005, they caught the answer: icequakes. Icebergs cracking and breaking off glaciers.
8. Later, a giant iceberg breaking apart near South Georgia made the same shape of sound.
9. No monster. Just ice. Gloom: "A bit disappointing. Mostly." *(Biscuit, relieved.)*

**New from the digest:** the 3,000 km distance, the submarine-hunting network, the location, the "far more powerful" quote, the monster theories, the scientist's ice hunch from the start, the South Georgia iceberg.
