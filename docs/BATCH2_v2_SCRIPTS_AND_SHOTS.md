# Batch 2 (eps 10-17) v2: scripts on the original mromidev formula + shot lists (for Andrew's OK)

Written 2026-11-26. Replaces the drafts in SCRIPTS_batch2.md. Same method as eps 2-9 v3: the original skill's formula, line by line, modelled on its example script (hook, setting, escalating beats, snap, "Luckily... / Unluckily...", "Wait...?", "You see," into an "Ever...?" analogy, consequence, callback that ends on the last word). Jokes are real facts from the sources. Each source was re-read in full first, and the digests are in shorts/digests/ep10-17_*.md (Oak Island also uses HISTORY and The Canadian Encyclopedia, because the Wikipedia page is thin on the pit). Nothing is built.

**Length:** 238-265 words each, 64-72 s at the skill's 3.7 words a second. Joe needs the same speed-up test as the other episodes.

**Shared shot rules:** the same as eps 2-9 v3 (kit models; a new place every ~4 s; push-ins and kicks; Gloom small and off to the side; one narrator, with Moth and Biscuit only as silent gags; captions with the key word in colour; night-blue light at "You see"). Music: Kevin MacLeod, CC BY, every title on the verified incompetech list.

Colour: oxidised copper green and CIA-night navy; night-blue from "You see". Music: "Cipher", then "Investigations" from shot 9.

### Script (260 words, ~70 s at 3.7 w/s)

It's 1990, in Langley, Virginia, and sculptor Jim Sanborn unveils Kryptos: a copper wall curled like printer paper, cut with nearly nine hundred letters. An NSA team cracks those three in 1992, and tells nobody. But the fourth section has never been solved. In 2006, Sanborn even admits a missing letter had sent everyone the wrong way. Luckily, he starts giving out clues. Unluckily, the clues are Berlin, clock, northeast and east. Wait, so the only person who knows the answer is the artist? In 2025, turning eighty, Sanborn says he no longer has the physical, mental or financial resources, and puts the solution up for auction. Then two writers find scraps of the answer in his own papers, in a Smithsonian archive. You see, a code is only as safe as the place its key is kept. Ever hidden a spare key under the doormat, and realised that's the first place everyone looks? Sanborn has those files sealed until 2075, and his archive sells for nine hundred sixty-two thousand dollars. So the CIA's own unsolved code finally slipped out, not to spies, but to two writers in an archive.

## EP 14: Sailing Stones (funny, mostly explained)
Colour: Death Valley ochre at dawn and icy blue; night-blue from "You see". Music: "Mesmerize", then "Ice Flow" from shot 11.

### Script (254 words, ~69 s at 3.7 w/s)
In Death Valley, rocks as heavy as a person slide across the desert, and for decades, nobody ever saw one move. It's Racetrack Playa, a dry lakebed flat as a table, and behind the rocks are tracks longer than a football field. In 1948, someone blames the wind. In 1955, a geologist says the big ones are far too heavy for wind, and suggests ice. In 1968, two geologists name thirty stones like pets: Mary Ann, Nancy, Karen. Nancy, smaller than a fist, travels two hundred and sixty metres. Karen, about three hundred and twenty kilos, vanishes, and turns up again in 1996. Still, nobody has seen a single rock move. Luckily, a camera study in 2009 works out how often they go. Unluckily, it says about once every three years, for ten seconds. Wait, so how do you catch a rock in the act? In 2013, a team puts GPS trackers in rocks and time-lapse cameras around the playa, and on December 20th, more than sixty rocks move at once. You see, when the playa floods in winter, a thin skin of ice forms overnight, and on sunny mornings it breaks into panels just millimetres thick. Ever watched ice on a puddle drift in the wind, pushing leaves along? Light wind pushes the panels, and the panels push the rocks, at up to five metres a minute. So the rocks aren't sailing, they're being bulldozed by ice. And today, the thing that moves them fastest isn't ice at all. It's people, stealing them.
