# Batch 1: scripts for episodes 2 to 9 (for approval)

Facts checked against Wikipedia and NOAA on 2026-11-25. Platform-neutral: no "subscribe" or "follow" lines, captions and key text kept in the centre safe area (works for YouTube Shorts, TikTok and Reels). Each is about 55 to 65 s. Word counts are approximate.

Series rules apply: attribute claims, no names of private people, no copied images, no jokes about deaths. "Funny" episodes use Gloom, Moth and Biscuit. "Informational" ones are Gloom only, neutral.

## EP 2: The Wow! Signal (funny)

**Hook.** One printout. One circled word. Forty-nine years of silence.

**Beat 1.** August 15, 1977. A radio telescope in Ohio, listening for aliens, picks up something. It lasts exactly 72 seconds.

**Beat 2.** Days later, a volunteer astronomer, Jerry Ehman, is reading the printout. He circles six characters, 6-E-Q-U-J-5, and writes one word beside them: "Wow!" *(Moth: "That's it? That's the whole reaction?")*

**Beat 3.** The characters aren't a message. They're loudness, one character every 10 seconds. The U means about 30 times louder than background noise. It rose, peaked in the middle, and faded, which is how a real source in the sky should look.

**Beat 4.** It sat near 1420 megahertz, the hydrogen line. Scientists had suggested that channel in 1959 as a sensible place to call another civilisation. It came from the direction of Sagittarius.

**Beat 5.** Nobody has heard it again. Searches in 1987, 1989, 1995 and 1999 found nothing. *(Biscuit stares at the sky.)*

**Beat 6.** Explanations: a reflected Earth signal, a one-off burst, a flickering signal. A 2017 comet idea was rejected by other astronomers. A 2024 preprint suggests a rare burst from a hydrogen cloud. Unconfirmed.

**Close.** Ehman said he would not draw "vast conclusions from half-vast data". Gloom: "Neither will I. Mostly."

**Sources:** Wikipedia "Wow! signal"; Ohio State Big Ear records.
**Check before render:** the exact frequency is given as 1420.3556 or 1420.4556 MHz depending on the source, so the script says only "near 1420 MHz".

## EP 3: Numbers Stations (informational)

**Hook.** Somewhere on the radio dial, a voice is reading out numbers. It has been doing it for decades.

**Beat 1.** These are shortwave radio stations. They broadcast groups of numbers, usually five digits, read by a recorded voice.

**Beat 2.** Many start with a signature. A tune, a word like "Attention", or a phrase. Then comes the count, then the numbers, then the same numbers again.

**Beat 3.** The leading explanation is a one-time pad. Each number group is a code that only the person with the matching pad can read. Done correctly, it can't be broken. Shortwave carries across continents, and anyone with a plain radio can listen without being traced.

**Beat 4.** The best-known British example played a folk song, "The Lincolnshire Poacher", then a female voice reading numbers. Its last recorded broadcast was in July 2008. It is believed, but not confirmed, to have been government-run.

**Beat 5.** Hobbyists log these stations and give them codes. In 2001, a US court heard prosecutors say that Cuban agents had decoded messages from one such station on handheld radios.

**Beat 6.** Governments rarely comment. Some have acknowledged old stations. Many stations still broadcast, and nobody official explains them.

**Close.** The numbers are public. The meaning isn't.

**Sources:** Wikipedia "Number station" and "Lincolnshire Poacher (numbers station)"; Priyom.org for live schedules.
**Check before render:** do not state who runs any station as fact.

## EP 4: Cicada 3301 (informational)

**Hook.** In January 2012, a plain image appeared on an anonymous message board. It asked for highly intelligent people.

**Beat 1.** Round one began January 4, 2012. The image hid data. Solving it led to another clue, then another.

**Beat 2.** The trail used images, a phone number, websites, and paper signs with QR codes. It referenced literature, poetry, art and music. Topics were cryptography, steganography and privacy.

**Beat 3.** Each message was signed with the same PGP key, so solvers could tell the real ones from fakes.

**Beat 4.** The puzzle came back in January 2013, and again in January 2014. The third round was never fully solved.

**Beat 5.** One solver said those who got far were asked about privacy and freedom of information, then invited to a private forum. What happened next is mostly unknown.

**Beat 6.** Who built it? Guesses range from an intelligence agency to a secret society to a game. Nobody has confirmed any of them.

**Beat 7.** Imitators appeared. In 2015, a group using "3301" attacked an organisation's database. The real signers posted a signed denial. The last verified signed message was in April 2017.

**Close.** The puzzle was built to find people. What it wanted them for is still unanswered.

**Sources:** Wikipedia "Cicada 3301".
**Check before render:** the first image's exact text and the real-world locations were not confirmed, so they are left out. Show no links or board names.

## EP 5: Max Headroom Hijack (funny)

**Hook.** November 22, 1987. Chicago TV got interrupted by someone who has never been caught.

**Beat 1.** About 9:14 p.m. A sports segment on one station. The screen goes black for around 15 seconds. *(Moth: "Is it supposed to do that?")*

**Beat 2.** Then a figure appears in a Max Headroom mask and sunglasses, swaying in front of a moving metal panel. No usable sound, only a buzz.

**Beat 3.** Engineers changed the frequency of the studio link and it stopped. The sports anchor said: "Well, if you're wondering what's happened, so am I." *(Biscuit approves.)*

**Beat 4.** About two hours later, it happened again on a public TV channel, at about 11:20 p.m. This time with sound. It lasted about 90 seconds, and viewers saw an odd, rambling bit of mischief. Nobody on duty could stop it. It ended when the pirates stopped transmitting.

**Beat 5.** How? A stronger microwave signal aimed at the stations' towers overrode the real one. It took skill and equipment, and it's very hard to do today.

**Beat 6.** The FCC investigated and found no culprit. The legal time limit ran out in 1992. Nobody has ever been identified.

**Close.** A masked figure took over two TV stations. And walked away.

**Sources:** Wikipedia "Max Headroom signal hijacking"; WTTW, Chicago Sun-Times anniversary pieces.
**Safety:** keep the second broadcast vague and clean. Do not describe its crude content.
**Check before render:** the length of the first broadcast varies by source (about 15 to 30 s), so say "about 15 seconds" for the blackout only.

## EP 6: The Dancing Plague of 1518 (informational)

**Hook.** July 1518. A woman starts dancing in a street in Strasbourg. She doesn't stop.

**Beat 1.** She danced for days, with no music, according to the accounts. Others joined. Within a week, dozens were dancing.

**Beat 2.** By August, estimates say between 50 and 400 people. The figure is uncertain.

**Beat 3.** Contemporary records, including a physician's notes, sermons and city council papers, do confirm the dancing.

**Beat 4.** The council's first response was to encourage it. Guild halls were set aside, with musicians. It backfired. They then banned public dancing and music, and sent dancers to a shrine.

**Beat 5.** Deaths are often reported, up to about 15 a day. But the Strasbourg sources from the time don't mention deaths. The claims come from later accounts.

**Beat 6.** Theories: poisoning from mouldy grain (ergot), stress-induced mass illness, religious belief. One historian rejects ergot, because poisoned people couldn't dance for days. Stress and mass illness is the most popular view.

**Close.** It faded in early September. Nobody knows why it began.

**Sources:** Wikipedia "Dancing plague of 1518"; Waller, The Lancet (2009).
**Check before render:** say "deaths are reported, but not in records from the time". No death toll on screen.

## EP 7: The Voynich Manuscript (informational)

**Hook.** A book with about 240 pages. Nobody on Earth can read it.

**Beat 1.** It's called the Voynich manuscript. It is held at Yale's Beinecke Library. It's named for a book dealer who bought it in 1912.

**Beat 2.** Carbon dating in 2009 put the parchment between 1404 and 1438. That dates the material, not the writing.

**Beat 3.** It's full of drawings. Plants no one can match to real ones. Circular charts of stars. Tiny figures bathing in green pools joined by pipes.

**Beat 4.** The text uses an unknown alphabet of roughly 20 to 25 main characters. Around 38,000 words, about 8,000 to 9,000 different ones.

**Beat 5.** It behaves like language. Word frequencies follow the same pattern as real languages. But it also has odd traits, like repeated words and a very predictable structure.

**Beat 6.** Theories: a cipher, an unknown language, an invented language, a hoax. Codebreakers from both world wars tried. None of the proposed solutions is accepted.

**Close.** It might say something. It might say nothing. Nobody has been able to prove which.

**Sources:** Wikipedia "Voynich manuscript"; Yale Beinecke Library.
**Check before render:** word and unique-word counts differ by source, so keep "about". Do not mention John Dee or Rudolf II (unverified).

## EP 8: Polybius (funny)

**Hook.** The deadliest arcade game ever. There's no proof it existed.

**Beat 1.** The story: 1981, Portland, Oregon. A black cabinet appears in arcades. Kids queue for it. *(Moth: "It sounds fun.")*

**Beat 2.** Then come the claims. Seizures, amnesia, nightmares. Men in black visit, collect data, and the machines vanish.

**Beat 3.** Gloom looks for evidence. The earliest web mention is an entry on a gaming site, dated 1998 on the page but showing 2000 in archives. The first print mention is a magazine in September 2003.

**Beat 4.** No cabinet. No chip. No 1980s article. No record of the German-sounding company named in the legend.

**Beat 5.** But some of it is real. In 1981, two players got sick at one Portland arcade. Ten days later, the FBI raided Portland arcades. The target was illegal gambling machines. *(Biscuit knocks a coin off the table.)*

**Beat 6.** One writer concluded it was "almost certainly invented". Games called Polybius do exist now, but they were made later.

**Close.** The cabinet never existed. The legend is doing very well.

**Sources:** Wikipedia "Polybius (urban legend)"; Skeptoid #362; Inverse (2020).
**Check before render:** "almost certainly invented" is Inverse's view, so attribute it.

## EP 9: The Bloop (funny, "mostly explained")

**Hook.** 1997. The ocean made a sound so loud that scientists wondered what could make it.

**Beat 1.** A US research agency, NOAA, was listening with underwater microphones, mainly for volcanoes.

**Beat 2.** It picked up an ultra-low rumble, rising in pitch over about a minute. It was loud enough to be heard on sensors thousands of kilometres apart. They traced it to the South Pacific. *(Moth hides.)*

**Beat 3.** It was named "the Bloop". The profile looked a little like an animal's call. But far too powerful for any animal on Earth. Internet theories: a giant creature, a secret test, a submarine.

**Beat 4.** NOAA's researchers ruled out a man-made source early. One suspected ice breaking off Antarctica.

**Beat 5.** Microphones moved closer to Antarctica. In 2005, the source was found. An icequake, an iceberg cracking and breaking away.

**Beat 6.** Similar sounds were later recorded from icebergs breaking up near South Georgia.

**Close.** No monster. Just ice. Gloom: "A bit disappointing. Mostly." *(Biscuit relieved.)*

**Sources:** NOAA Ocean Service "bloop" page; Wikipedia "Bloop".
**Check before render:** distances differ by source, so say "thousands of kilometres". I did not use any Lovecraft link because it isn't documented.
