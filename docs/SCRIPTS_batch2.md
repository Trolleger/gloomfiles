# Batch 2: scripts for episodes 10 to 17 (for approval)

Facts checked against Wikipedia, NOAA/NPS, Scientific American and the CIA site on 2026-11-25. Same rules as batch 1: platform-neutral, no subscribe lines, centre safe area, claims attributed. Each script is about 150 spoken words. Funny ones use Gloom, Moth and Biscuit.

## EP 10: Kryptos (informational)

**Hook.** There is a sculpture outside CIA headquarters with four coded messages. One of them took 35 years.

**Beat 1.** Kryptos was dedicated in 1990 at CIA headquarters in Virginia. The sculptor, Jim Sanborn, worked with a retiring CIA cryptographer on the codes. The name is Greek for "hidden".

**Beat 2.** Its copper screen carries 869 characters of ciphertext, in four passages. The first three were solved in the 1990s. An NSA team was first, a CIA analyst second, and a computer scientist was first to publish.

**Beat 3.** The fourth, K4, is 97 characters long. It resisted everyone.

**Beat 4.** The sculptor released clues. In 2010, the letters "BERLIN". In 2014, "CLOCK". In 2020, "NORTHEAST".

**Beat 5.** In 2025, two writers found scraps of the original plaintext in the sculptor's papers at the Smithsonian. The sculptor confirmed it was authentic, and then had the files sealed until 2075.

**Close.** The words are known. The method still isn't. So is it solved?

**Sources:** Scientific American, "A Solution to the CIA's Kryptos Code Is Found after 35 Years"; CIA.gov Kryptos page; Wikipedia "Kryptos".
**Check before render:** do NOT say the code was "cracked". The accurate line is that the plaintext was found in archive scraps and confirmed. The CIA page still says K4 is unbroken. A fifth passage is said to exist, but leave it out.

## EP 11: The Backrooms (funny)

**Hook.** A photo of an empty yellow office. Seven years later it has its own movie.

**Beat 1.** May 12, 2019. On an anonymous paranormal board, someone asks for pictures that "feel off". They post a big, empty, yellow room. *(Moth: "It's an office.")*

**Beat 2.** Another anonymous user writes a short description. If you fall out of reality in the wrong place, you land in the Backrooms. The smell of old carpet. The buzz of lights. Roughly six hundred million square miles of empty rooms.

**Beat 3.** Nobody's name is on it. Writers added levels and monsters. A forum grew past 157,000 members by 2022. *(Biscuit watches a hallway.)*

**Beat 4.** In January 2022, a 16-year-old uploaded a short found-footage video, made in Blender and After Effects over about a month. It grew into a series.

**Beat 5.** Where was the photo from? In 2024, fans traced it to a 2002 snapshot on a store renovation blog in Wisconsin. The room is now an RC car track.

**Close.** The scariest place on the internet was a furniture store. Gloom: "Aisle three, probably."

**Sources:** Wikipedia "The Backrooms".
**Check before render:** keep the board and the original post unlinked. Don't name the 16-year-old. Film release details are less certain, so leave the film out.

## EP 12: The Buzzer, UVB-76 (informational)

**Hook.** On 4625 kilohertz, a buzzer has sounded for decades. Sometimes a voice reads words.

**Beat 1.** It's a shortwave radio signal. About 25 buzzes a minute, around the clock. It has been on air since about the mid-1970s. The earliest preserved recording is from 1982.

**Beat 2.** Hobbyists call it "the Buzzer". For years it only buzzed. The first voice message was on December 24, 1997.

**Beat 3.** The voice reads a callsign, then a few words and numbers. The callsign has changed several times since.

**Beat 4.** On December 11, 2024, it sent 24 messages with 30 different words in one day. A monitoring group called it the most talkative day in the station's history.

**Beat 5.** Pirates sometimes break in with music or speech. Those events aren't the station itself.

**Beat 6.** Who runs it? The Russian military is the common assumption. That's never been officially confirmed. A "doomsday signal" theory is widely considered unlikely.

**Close.** The signal is real. The purpose is not public.

**Sources:** Wikipedia "UVB-76"; Meduza, "Stay tuned" (Dec 17, 2024).
**Check before render:** say "the best-supported idea", not fact. Start date is disputed (1976 to 1982), so use "mid-1970s, earliest recording 1982". Skip the 2022 messages.

## EP 13: The Tully Monster (funny)

**Hook.** A fossil so strange scientists still can't agree what it was.

**Beat 1.** 1955, Illinois. An amateur collector finds a fossil and takes it to the Field Museum. The experts are stumped. *(Moth: "A bit of a theme.")*

**Beat 2.** It's about 300 million years old. Up to 35 centimetres long. A cigar-shaped body, a tail fin, and eyes on two stalks. And a long snout with a tiny claw at the end.

**Beat 3.** It was named in 1966. In 1989, Illinois made it the state fossil. *(Biscuit approves.)*

**Beat 4.** In 2016, two papers said it was a vertebrate, related to fish like lampreys. In 2017, another team replied that it was not.

**Beat 5.** Since then: chemistry studies lean toward a chordate. A 2023 scan study doubts several of the vertebrate features. Still unsettled.

**Close.** Thousands of fossils. One verdict, missing. Gloom: "Science is thorough. Eventually."

**Sources:** Wikipedia "Tully Monster"; Penn Today (Feb 23, 2017).
**Check before render:** age is given as 300 to 310 million years. Say "about 300 million". Latest status comes from Wikipedia's summary, so say "still debated".

## EP 14: Sailing Stones of Racetrack Playa (funny, "mostly explained")

**Hook.** Rocks in Death Valley move on their own. For decades, nobody saw it happen.

**Beat 1.** Racetrack Playa is a flat, dry lake bed in California. Rocks sit on it with long trails behind them. The first scientific reports date from 1948. *(Moth: "Who's pushing them?")*

**Beat 2.** Theories: dust devils, gale winds, slippery algae, thick sheets of ice. Nobody had a recording. Trails showed up, but the rocks never moved while anyone watched.

**Beat 3.** In 2011, researchers put GPS trackers in 15 rocks, and added a weather station and cameras. One researcher expected "the most boring experiment ever".

**Beat 4.** After a rare winter storm, a shallow pond formed. On December 20, 2013, more than 60 rocks moved.

**Beat 5.** The answer: thin ice, 3 to 6 millimetres thick. At sunrise it breaks into panels. A light wind pushes the panels, and the panels push the rocks, a few metres a minute.

**Close.** No ghosts. A gentle breeze and some ice. Gloom: "Very disappointing. Very satisfying." *(Biscuit pushes a mug off the desk.)*

**Sources:** Norris et al., PLOS ONE (Aug 27, 2014); NPS Death Valley release.
**Check before render:** the heaviest rocks (reported up to 320 kg by the National Park Service) have not been observed moving. Say "mostly explained". The test rocks were smaller.

## EP 15: Candle Cove (funny)

**Hook.** A kids' show everyone remembers. It never existed.

**Beat 1.** 2009. A short story appears online, written as a forum thread. Adults trade memories of a children's show called Candle Cove, on Channel 58, in the early 1970s.

**Beat 2.** The show: a girl named Janice and her imaginary adventures with marionette pirates. *(Moth: "I don't remember it at all.")*

**Beat 3.** Then the memories turn strange. A skeleton pirate villain. An episode where the puppets only scream.

**Beat 4.** The ending: one man says his mother told him that when he watched Candle Cove, the TV just showed static. *(Biscuit stares at a static screen.)*

**Beat 5.** It's fiction, by a web cartoonist named Kris Straub. People copied it onto forums, and some fans staged the conversation as though it had really happened. A TV series followed in 2016.

**Close.** There was never a show. There was only static. Gloom: "Which is also the case with most daytime TV."

**Sources:** Wikipedia "Candle Cove".
**Check before render:** the author is public and credited. Skip the skin-based detail of the villain (child-related). I couldn't verify that large numbers of people were actually fooled, so the script doesn't claim it.

## EP 16: The Oak Island Money Pit (informational)

**Hook.** A hole in Nova Scotia. People have been digging it for over 230 years.

**Beat 1.** Oak Island sits in Mahone Bay. The story starts in 1795, when a teenager reportedly found a dip in the ground near a large oak tree.

**Beat 2.** Early accounts describe a shaft with oak-log platforms every ten feet. These come from accounts written decades later. Not documents.

**Beat 3.** In 1804, diggers reached about 30 metres. The shaft flooded with sea water. Later digs found tunnels linking the pit to the ocean.

**Beat 4.** One account says a stone found deep in the pit read: "Ten feet below two million lies buried." The stone is lost. The reading is one decoder's.

**Beat 5.** A 1971 camera showed faint images that some thought were chests. Unverified.

**Beat 6.** Skeptics point to the island's limestone, which can form sinkholes that fill with silt and wood.

**Close.** After more than 230 years: no treasure confirmed. No one has stopped looking.

**Sources:** The Canadian Encyclopedia "Oak Island"; HISTORY "History of Oak Island".
**Check before render:** the detailed early story is told "by accounts". No deaths mentioned (sources disagree on the count). Don't mention the TV show's claimed finds as fact.

## EP 17: The Hum (informational)

**Hook.** A low sound that some people hear, and others never do.

**Beat 1.** The Hum is a persistent low-frequency drone reported in towns around the world. Only some people nearby can hear it.

**Beat 2.** In the early 1990s, a survey in Taos, New Mexico, found about 2 percent of residents were hearers. Their descriptions varied. Instruments in their homes found nothing unusual.

**Beat 3.** A 1995 study reported hearers heard it at different frequencies, between 32 and 80 hertz.

**Beat 4.** Some hums were traced. Windsor, Ontario, had reports from 2011. They were linked to blast furnaces on a nearby island, and faded after they shut down in April 2020. In Kokomo, Indiana, two industrial sources were found. Reports continued after they were fixed.

**Beat 5.** Other theories: tinnitus, unusually sensitive hearing, or focusing on ordinary sounds. One researcher estimated about a third of cases were physical and two thirds were not.

**Close.** There isn't one Hum. There are several, and some have no known cause.

**Sources:** Wikipedia "The Hum"; Live Science "What is the Taos Hum?".
**Check before render:** the Windsor source is a likely link, because officials were denied access to the island. The two-thirds estimate is one researcher's. Skip the reported suicides.
